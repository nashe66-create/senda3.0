# Senda Market Intelligence Report
## UK → Africa Corridor Screening — September 2026

### Executive summary

Senda's secondary research has now screened eight Flutterwave-compatible destination corridors from the UK: Nigeria, Kenya, Zambia, South Africa, Uganda, Tanzania, Rwanda and Ghana.

The infrastructure question is substantially clearer. Flutterwave's current transfer documentation supports all eight destinations for payouts, with bank-transfer rails across the relevant local currencies and mobile-money rails in Ghana, Kenya, Rwanda, Tanzania, Uganda and Zambia. Flutterwave supports individual and bulk transfers from available balance. This makes the technical payout side of Senda's grouped-transfer model plausible, subject to Flutterwave confirming the exact UK collection, settlement, compliance and commercial structure for Senda.

The market question is also clearer. All eight corridors have established remittance competition, and several have providers offering low-cost digital transfers, bank deposits, cash pickup and/or mobile wallets. Senda therefore should not position itself primarily as another cheaper or faster remittance provider.

The unresolved customer problem is more specific: how common and valuable is it for UK-based remitters to manage multiple regular recipients/commitments, and would they prefer to create one Remittance Order, make one payment and then track multiple recipient payouts independently?

The evidence supports continuing the UK → Nigeria pilot while validating Kenya and Ghana strongly, and keeping Uganda, Tanzania and Zambia in the next research pool. South Africa and Rwanda should remain hypotheses to test rather than immediate expansion priorities. These are research priorities, not a prediction of commercial success.

---

## 1. Senda's proposition being tested

Senda is not primarily trying to improve the mechanics of sending one transfer.

The product hypothesis is:

> One Remittance Order → one customer payment → multiple independently tracked recipient payouts.

A customer may have several recurring commitments: parents, siblings, children, school fees, household support or other obligations. Senda groups these into one order while retaining separate recipient-level records and payout status.

The critical evidence gap is therefore behavioural, not technological.

We need to establish:

- how many regular recipients typical remitters support;
- how many separate transfers they make in a normal period;
- whether they repeat similar transfers regularly;
- whether they currently track those payments manually;
- what causes the greatest friction;
- whether one grouped order and one payment is materially better for them;
- what trust or regulatory concerns would prevent adoption.

## 2. Flutterwave compatibility

Flutterwave currently documents transfers to Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, South Africa, Rwanda and Zambia. Bank-transfer documentation lists NGN, GHS, KES, TZS, UGX, ZAR and ZMW and provides country-specific transfer guidance. Mobile-money documentation lists Ghana, Kenya, Rwanda, Tanzania, Uganda and Zambia. Flutterwave also supports single and bulk transfers from available balance.

This is important because Senda does not need the payment provider to understand Senda's customer-facing grouping as a single payout. Senda can maintain the Remittance Order and individual Transfers in its own system, while using Flutterwave for the underlying collection/settlement and payout rails if the commercial and regulatory model is approved.

What remains unconfirmed:

1. UK customer collection for Senda's proposed remittance model.
2. Whether Flutterwave can support the required settlement/funding flow.
3. Whether one customer collection can appropriately fund several independent payouts under Senda's account structure.
4. FX treatment and locked quote mechanics.
5. Senda's required regulatory permissions/licensing or partnership structure.
6. Transaction limits, reserves, safeguarding and settlement timing.
7. Required KYC/KYB and transaction-monitoring responsibilities between Flutterwave and Senda.

These are now partnership/compliance questions rather than reasons to abandon the technical model.

## 4. UK → Nigeria

### Market

Nigeria is the current Senda pilot corridor. It combines significant UK-linked diaspora activity, substantial remittance demand and a well-developed digital remittance market.

The Migration Observatory reports a historical 2021 UK-to-Nigeria bilateral remittance estimate of approximately £2.76bn. Bilateral estimates should not be presented as current 2026 flows because comparable official bilateral data were discontinued after 2021.

### Provider landscape

The corridor is highly competitive. Current alternatives include Wise, WorldRemit, Remitly, Sendwave, Western Union, MoneyGram, TransferGo and others. Provider-level World Bank observations show that some digital providers can have very low benchmark costs.

### Payout methods

Flutterwave supports NGN bank transfers. Market alternatives also provide bank deposit, cash pickup and mobile-wallet options.

### Regulatory considerations

Nigeria has an active CBN framework for International Money Transfer Operators. In March 2026, the CBN reiterated requirements under the revised IMTO guidelines, including designated naira settlement accounts with authorised dealer banks for remittance transactions.

### Senda opportunity

Price alone is unlikely to provide a defensible differentiation. The strongest hypothesis is the management of multiple family commitments within one grouped order.

### Research requirement

Very high. Nigeria should be the first corridor for the short behavioural survey.

## 5. UK → Kenya

### Market

Kenya has a substantial UK diaspora and a mature digital/mobile-money ecosystem.

The World Bank's Q3 2025 UK→Kenya average total cost was 2.63%. Provider-level observations included materially lower costs.

### Payout methods

Flutterwave supports KES bank transfers and M-Pesa. This is particularly relevant to Senda because a single grouped order could theoretically contain bank and mobile-money recipients, subject to the final provider and compliance structure.

### Regulatory considerations

The Central Bank of Kenya licenses money remittance providers and maintains a current directory. Kenya's regulatory framework explicitly covers money remittance and payment services.

### Senda opportunity

The combination of strong digital payments, mobile-money usage and UK diaspora makes Kenya a useful second validation market. The question is whether multi-recipient family support is common enough to justify a corridor-specific proposition.

### Research requirement

High.

---

## 6. UK → Ghana

### Market

Ghana has a significant UK-linked migrant population and established remittance flows. The Migration Observatory reports a 2024 average UK sending cost of 5.1%, while the World Bank's Q3 2025 corridor average was 3.79%.

### Payout methods

Flutterwave supports GHS bank transfers and mobile money, including MTN and Telecel/AirtelTigo-related rails documented in its mobile-money material.

### Provider landscape

Remitly, WorldRemit, Wise, Western Union, MoneyGram and other providers compete on the route.

### Regulatory considerations

The Bank of Ghana publishes specific guidelines for International Money Transfer Operators and has recently reminded the market that unapproved MTO activity is a regulatory concern.

### Senda opportunity

Ghana's combination of established remittance behaviour and mobile-money infrastructure makes it useful for testing whether Senda's grouped-recipient model travels beyond Nigeria.

### Research requirement

High enough to include in the first extended research wave.

## 7. UK → Uganda

Flutterwave supports UGX bank transfers and Airtel/MTN mobile money. World Bank Q3 2025 average cost was 3.66%; the Migration Observatory reported 5.0% for its 2024 £120 benchmark.

WorldRemit and Mukuru already provide UK-to-Uganda services, so basic transfer functionality is crowded.

The Senda hypothesis is strongest if UK-based Ugandan remitters regularly support several people or obligations and need a better way to organise them.

**Research priority: medium-high.**

---

## 8. UK → Tanzania

Flutterwave supports TZS bank transfers and mobile-money rails including Airtel, HaloPesa, Tigo and Vodacom. World Bank Q3 2025 average cost was 4.35%; the Migration Observatory reported 5.7% for 2024.

WorldRemit and Remitly provide established UK-to-Tanzania options with bank, cash and mobile-wallet delivery.

The market is therefore relevant but competitive. The customer research should specifically test whether users manage multiple recurring recipients rather than simply asking whether they send money internationally.

**Research priority: medium-high.**

---

## 9. UK → Zambia

Flutterwave supports ZMW bank transfers and mobile money. World Bank Q3 2025 average cost was 2.89%, while the Migration Observatory reported a 7.9% average for its 2024 £120 benchmark.

Mukuru and WorldRemit already provide UK-to-Zambia services, including mobile-wallet and cash options.

The cost difference between datasets is itself a reason to avoid simplistic pricing claims and to collect live quotes during later competitive research.

The addressable UK diaspora is smaller than Nigeria/Kenya/Ghana, so customer behaviour around multiple recipients needs to justify additional product investment.

**Research priority: medium-high.**

---

## 10. UK → South Africa

Flutterwave supports ZAR bank transfers.

The World Bank Q3 2025 average cost was 4.64%, while the Migration Observatory reported 7.2% for its 2024 £120 benchmark.

The provider market includes Wise, WorldRemit, Mukuru, Ria, MoneyGram and Western Union. South African users may include a larger share of bank-to-bank or personal transfer use cases than some other African corridors, so the customer segment needs to be identified rather than assuming all transfers are family support.

**Research priority: medium.**

---

## 11. UK → Rwanda

Flutterwave documents Rwanda payout support and mobile money through M-Pesa and MTN. The World Bank Q3 2025 average cost was 7.43%, with significant provider-level dispersion; the Migration Observatory reported 7.9% for its 2024 benchmark.

The National Bank of Rwanda's licensed-institutions list includes remittance providers and Flutterwave Rwanda as an e-money issuer, showing that the country has a formal regulated payments ecosystem.

The higher average transfer cost creates a potential customer pain point, but the smaller UK addressable population means customer validation should precede significant expansion work.

**Research priority: medium.**

## 12. Competitive conclusion

The research does not support building Senda around:

- cheaper transfers alone;
- faster transfers alone;
- basic transfer tracking alone;
- access to mobile money alone;
- access to bank payouts alone.

Those features already exist across the market.

The more differentiated hypothesis is **financial commitment management for remitters**.

Senda's potential product advantage is the layer above the payment rail:

**Commitments → Remittance Order → one payment → individual Transfers → recipient-level tracking/reconciliation.**

That is the proposition we now need customers to validate.

---

## 13. Regulatory conclusion

Senda should not assume that using Flutterwave automatically makes Senda's activity unregulated.

The FCA states that receiving customer money before passing it to another party can constitute a regulated payment service, including money remittance. Firms providing payment services as a regular business generally need the appropriate authorisation or registration unless an exclusion applies.

Therefore, before live customer funds are handled, Senda needs a confirmed operating model covering:

- UK regulatory status;
- safeguarding/holding of funds;
- customer-money flow;
- Flutterwave's role;
- settlement ownership;
- KYC/KYB;
- AML transaction monitoring;
- sanctions screening;
- complaints and consumer protection;
- destination-country licensing/partner requirements;
- reconciliation and payout failure handling.

The appropriate next step is to obtain Flutterwave's partnership/compliance view and, separately, specialist UK regulatory advice.

## 14. What the secondary research tells us

The secondary research is now sufficiently developed to move into targeted primary research.

We do **not** need a large generic survey.

We need a short behavioural survey aimed at people who actually remit from the UK to Nigeria first, with a smaller extension to Kenya and Ghana if useful.

The survey should establish:

1. Do you send money internationally?
2. Where do you send it?
3. How often?
4. How many regular recipients do you support?
5. How many separate payments did you make the last time you sent money?
6. What were the payments for?
7. How do you currently keep track of them?
8. What is the most annoying part?
9. Would one order containing several recipients and one payment be useful?
10. What would make you trust/use such a service?
11. Would you be interested in trying it?

Pricing should not be the main focus of the first survey. We first need to prove the problem exists.

## Important evidence limitations

- Bilateral UK remittance estimates by destination are not consistently available after 2021.
- World Bank corridor prices are dated survey observations, not live consumer quotes.
- Provider pricing and FX rates change frequently.
- Diaspora size is not the same as number of active remitters.
- Public datasets generally do not reveal the number of recipients supported per remitter.
- Public provider pages establish that functionality exists but do not prove that customers want Senda's grouped-recipient workflow.

**The next decision should therefore be driven by customer behaviour evidence, not by market size alone.**
